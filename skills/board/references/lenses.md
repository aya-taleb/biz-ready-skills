# The lenses

Each lens is a published business framework, summarized in our own words. The board uses
the framework's questions. It never speaks as the author, quotes the author about this
idea, or suggests the author has seen or endorses anything.

Score each lens 1 to 5:
- 5: strong evidence it holds
- 4: likely holds, some evidence
- 3: plausible, no evidence yet
- 2: real doubts the founder has not answered
- 1: a known reason it fails

A score of 3 means "untested", not "average". Most new ideas have several 3s, and that is
fine. The conditions are how they move.

---

## Demand: Jobs to be Done

Source: Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan, *Competing
Against Luck* (2016).

The idea: people "hire" a product to make progress in a specific situation. The rival is
whatever they hire today, including nothing.

Ask:
- What is the situation, in one sentence ("Tuesday, 12:10, back-to-back meetings, hungry")?
- What progress does the buyer want, and what is in the way?
- What do they hire today, and what do they fire it for?
- What would make them switch: the push of the old way, the pull of the new, against the
  habit and the worry about switching?

A high score needs a specific situation and a clear reason to fire the current solution.

## Evidence: riskiest assumption first

Sources: Ash Maurya, *Running Lean* (2nd ed. 2012); Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013).

The idea: every plan rests on a few assumptions. Find the one that would sink it if wrong
and test it cheaply first. Opinions and compliments are not evidence; past behavior and
money are.

Ask:
- What has the founder seen people DO (pay, pre-order, switch, complain in public)?
- What is the one assumption that, if false, ends the idea?
- What is the cheapest test that could prove it false within two weeks?

A high score needs behavior or money, not "people said they'd love it".

## Money: unit economics and the engine of growth

Sources: Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011), the three engines of growth (sticky, viral,
paid); contribution margin from standard management accounting.

The idea: each sale must leave money after its own costs, and the business needs one engine
that brings the next customer at a cost it can afford.

Ask:
- What does one sale leave after its direct costs (ingredients, delivery, payment fees,
  hosting)? A number or `{{your number}}`.
- Which engine: repeat buyers (sticky), buyers who bring buyers (viral), or paid ads and
  sales (paid)? What does the founder know about it?
- Roughly how many sales a month cover the fixed costs? If the founder cannot say, the
  condition is "run founder-cfo".

Never invent the numbers. A 3 here usually means "no numbers yet".

## Reach: the channel test

Source: Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015), the Bullseye framework.

The idea: most ideas die from no reach, not a bad product. Pick a few channels, test them
cheaply, focus on the one that works.

Ask:
- Where do these buyers already gather (a place, a group, a search, a feed, an office)?
- Which two or three channels could reach the first 100 buyers, and which can the founder
  run alone?
- What would a cheap test of each look like?

## Durability (pick one by business type)

### For apps, software and online products: 7 Powers

Source: Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers* (2016).

The idea: lasting profit needs a barrier: scale economies, network effects, counter-
positioning, switching costs, branding, a cornered resource, or process power.

Ask: which, if any, could this business build in three years? Which rival could copy it
fastest? Early ideas rarely have one yet; say which one is plausible.

### For local, service and physical businesses: Five Forces

Source: Michael E. Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy," *Harvard Business
Review* (1979), and *Competitive Strategy* (1980).

The idea: profit depends on rivalry, the power of buyers and suppliers, the threat of new
entrants and substitutes.

Ask: how easy is it to open the same thing next door? Who sets prices, the buyer or a
supplier (a landlord, a delivery app taking a commission)? What substitutes are close?

## The pre-mortem

Source: Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem," *Harvard Business Review* (2007).

The idea: imagine it failed, then explain why. People find more real risks this way than by
listing risks forward.

Write: "It is <date one year from today>. <Business> closed. Here is what happened." Then
two or three short, specific stories. Each one becomes a kill risk with a sign to watch for.

## Which lenses to use

| business | lenses |
| --- | --- |
| app, software, online product | Demand, Evidence, Money, Reach, 7 Powers |
| local shop, food, service, trades | Demand, Evidence, Money, Reach, Five Forces |
| very early (one sentence idea, no customers) | Demand, Evidence, Money, then the pre-mortem; skip durability and say why |

The pre-mortem runs every time.
