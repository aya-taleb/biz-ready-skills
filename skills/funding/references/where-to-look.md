# Where to look for funding

These are starting points to search, not a list of programs to recommend. Programs open,
close, change amounts and shut down. Open each page on the day you use it, read the
current terms, and write the date you saw them. If a page says a program is paused, closed
or "coming soon", say that.

Every link below is a hub or directory you search from. The skill never names a program
it did not find on a live page during this session.

## US federal R&D grants (non-dilutive)

- **SBIR and STTR**: federal R&D money for small US businesses doing technical work with a
  commercial path. Hub: https://www.sbir.gov/ (agencies, topics, how to apply).
  - Fits: new technology with real R&D risk (software counts if the technical problem is
    hard and new, not a plain app on known tools). Not a fit for a café, a store, an agency
    or a standard SaaS with no research question.
  - Each agency runs its own program and calendar. Common ones to check: NSF
    (https://seedfund.nsf.gov/, which asks for a short Project Pitch first), NIH, DoD,
    DOE, USDA, NASA. Find their current pages from sbir.gov.
  - STTR needs a partner research institution (a university or lab).
  - The programs were reauthorized in 2026 after a lapse, with new rules (for example
    proposal caps per company set by each agency). Read the current rules on sbir.gov and
    the agency page before planning on a date.
  - Free help: many states run SBIR assistance offices; search sbir.gov for local help.
- **Grants.gov** (https://www.grants.gov/): search for federal grant listings. Most are for
  nonprofits, schools and governments, so check the "eligible applicants" field first.

## State and local

- State economic development or commerce departments, and city small business offices.
  Search "<state> department of commerce small business grant" and the city's site.
- Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs): free advising, and they know local grants and
  loans. Find one at https://americassbdc.org/.
- SBA (https://www.sba.gov/): loans (7(a), microloans) and guides. The SBA itself rarely
  gives grants to start a business; say so if a user expects one.
- Local business pitch competitions, chambers of commerce and community foundations.
  These are small and change every year. Search for this year's page.

## Accelerators

- Search for accelerators that fit the stage, sector and place. Read each one's current
  page for: deadline, batch dates, amount, equity taken, location, and whether it is
  remote.
- Examples of the kinds that exist: large general ones (such as Y Combinator, Techstars),
  sector ones (health, climate, food), university and regional ones, and ones for
  under-represented founders. Name only those you found on a live page today.
- Read the deal terms page. Note the equity and any fees. A program that charges the
  founder to join is a red flag; say so.

## Angels and pre-seed funds

- Angel groups and networks by region (search "<city> angel investors group").
- Pre-seed funds that publish a thesis matching the business.
- Platforms where founders list a round. Check terms and fees.
- For angels and funds there is often no deadline. Write "rolling".
- Do not list individual angels by name unless they publicly invite pitches on their own
  page. Never pull personal contact details.

## Other non-dilutive

- Revenue-based financing, community loans (CDFIs), crowdfunding (reward or equity).
  Each has costs; say what they are from the provider's page.
- Corporate programs and credits (cloud credits are not cash; say so).

## For local and service businesses

Most local businesses (food, retail, services) will not fit SBIR, accelerators or angels.
Their real options are usually: savings, a small loan (SBA microloan, CDFI, a bank), local
grants and pitch competitions, pre-sales to customers, and a landlord build-out allowance.
Say this plainly. It is better than sending a café owner to apply for venture money.
